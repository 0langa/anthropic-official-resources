The Creative Value Lens · 10 min

SummaryTranscript

Creative work requires a wider set of competencies and qualities than traditional knowledge work. This module introduces the Creative value lens. It has three dimensions, creative expression, human connection, and distinctiveness, and will help you analyze what your creative work is for, and what kinds of value it produces. The three dimensions operate concurrently. Any creative work can be analyzed through all three at once, and no dimension takes precedence over the others. I'll walk you through each dimension, talk about how they interact and why different creators weigh them differently.

Expression value has two parts. The creator experiences something compelling in the act of making, and the audience experiences something compelling in engaging with the work. Most creative practices include work across a range of expression intensity. High expression, where producing a compelling response is the primary goal. Medium expression, where expressive impact is balanced with functional or commercial goals. And low expression, where clarity, accuracy, or function are the goals.

Consider a creative who illustrates children's books and designs product packaging in the same week. Children's book work is high expression. Product packaging is medium expression. The illustrator's job, standards of excellence, and AI involvement are likely different in each case. The lens is not asking you to rank your work or apologize for the low expression parts. The low expression parts of your practice may be vital to your overall goals as a creator, just as much as the high expression parts. The lens is asking you to see which level of expression you're using because the analysis of AI's role depends on it.

Connection lives between people rather than inside the work itself, and it depends on the audience's perception of who made the work and why. It rewards authenticity and is damaged by deception. High connection is when the audience's sense of a specific human behind the work is central. Medium connection is when the human presence is felt, but diffused through teams, brands, or conventions. And low connection is when the creator is anonymous, interchangeable, or irrelevant to the audience's experience.

Think about a writer working on a personal essay. Their whole point is to give the audience a sense that a specific person is telling them something true about their experience. The reader is in relationship with the writer, mediated by the prose. That is high connection. Now, think about the same writer drafting a project proposal for a grant. You can sense the writer in the proposal, but the goal is to evaluate the project, not to feel the writer. That is medium connection. The same writer in the same week works across different connection profiles, which means the questions about AI's role are different for each piece.

Let's talk about authenticity anxiety. Lots of creators are asking the question, "If AI touched it, is it still mine?" Or their audiences are asking, "If you used AI, is it still yours?" The answer has two parts.

The first part is about the work itself. Authenticity is a function of decisions, techniques, and expression. If the final work reflects your decisions throughout and you can describe your process honestly, you are more likely to feel that the work is authentically yours. If you accepted what AI produced without serious engagement, the work has different authenticity than work you wrestled into being.

The second part is about connection to the work. Even if the work is authentically yours, your audience's experience of it depends on what they perceive. If they expect a human presence in the work and AI involvement changes the nature of that presence in ways the audience would care about, that matters for connection. The connection dimension is the one most directly affected by how your AI use is perceived.

The third lens is distinctiveness. A work has distinctiveness value when it's unlike other work. And distinctiveness has two components. First, there's the concept of originality, novelty in content or in execution. And then there's specificity, particular characteristics of execution. Distinctiveness is not just about being first or being different.

Think about a new production of Hamlet. It's not a new concept, but the specific costume, set, lighting, and performance decisions can constitute a distinct realization. Distinctiveness lives in the specificity of decisions, rather than only in conceptual novelty. High distinctiveness is highly unique or serving a very specific purpose, audience, or context. While medium distinctiveness has established forms with broader appeal and more competition. And low distinctiveness is intentionally standardized or interchangeable.

Distinctiveness matters for AI decisions because AI tools tend to converge outputs across users. Two creators using the same model with similar prompts get similar results. That convergence is sometimes acceptable and sometimes corrosive. And the lens helps you see which of your work depends on distinctiveness and which does not.

The three dimensions can reinforce each other or pull against each other. A creative decision that maximizes distinctiveness might reduce accessibility, which can reduce connection or expression for some audiences.

Consider a graphic designer working on a poster for a community arts festival. A design that maximizes distinctiveness through unfamiliar visual choices may lose the community's sense that the poster speaks to them. While a design that maximizes connection through familiar local imagery may lose distinctiveness. The lens does not tell the designer which way to go. It surfaces the trade-off, so the designer can make the choice deliberately.

The value lens describes what your work is for, and the three dimensions of expression, connection, and distinctiveness give you vocabulary for the kinds of value your creative practice produces. The next module introduces the second analytical tool for creatives, the production lens. The value lens describes what your work is for, and the production lens describes what it costs to make.
