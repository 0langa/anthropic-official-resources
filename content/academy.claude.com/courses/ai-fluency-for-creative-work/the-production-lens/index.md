## Transcript

Welcome back. The previous module introduced the creative value lens, which gives you vocabulary for the kinds of value your work produces. This module introduces the second analytical tool, the production lens, which describes what it costs to create and gives you vocabulary for the practical factors that shape how the work gets made.

AI tools are commonly sold as constraint busters – faster, cheaper, easier. Sometimes that description is accurate, but the real world is rarely that clear. It's more likely that the tools shift constraints rather than removing them, or sometimes they can add new constraints. The production lens helps you see what is actually happening for your specific work.

The lens has two components: production constraints and technological complexity. And the two are structurally related. Technology affects production constraints. It always has with every significant tool change in creative work in the past. The printing press changed the constraints for reproducing text. Digital photography changed the production constraints of taking pictures from rolls of film to computer memory. AI is the current instance of that pattern.

Production constraints are the baseline costs of making creative work. They come in three categories: time, effort, and resources. Consider an independent game developer working on a narrative game. Time is years from concept to shipped game. Effort spans the full range, from the cognitive work of system design, to the emotional work of writing characters, to the labor of actually building levels. Resources include software licenses, possibly contractor fees for art or audio, and the opportunity cost of not doing other paid work.

The three constraints interact constantly. More money might buy back some time through hiring help. More skill, which is a form of accumulated effort, might reduce both time and resource needs.

Technology affects production constraints. Sometimes it lowers them, sometimes it raises them, and sometimes it shifts constraints from one category to another. This pattern is not new, and it is not specific to AI.

Consider digital recording in music production. Recording used to require expensive studio time, expensive equipment, and specialized engineering skills. Digital tools brought the resource cost of recording down dramatically. A musician can now record an album in a bedroom with a laptop and a microphone for a fraction of what professional studio time would cost. The resource constraint moved significantly.

But the constraints did not vanish. The musician now needs to learn digital audio software, which takes time and effort. They need to do their own mixing and mastering, which used to be specialized labor handled by experienced engineers. The skill required for those stages is real, and getting good takes years. Some of the resource constraints that came off the studio cost moved into effort constraints around new skill development, and time constraints around handling more stages of production personally. The pattern is that technology rarely just reduces constraints, it rearranges them. Understanding this pattern is the right baseline for analyzing AI.

AI is a current and significant instance of technological complexity affecting production constraints. AI is making some technologically complex production tasks cheaper, quicker, and more accessible. Code generation, draft text production, image generation, audio generation, transcription, summarization, formatting, and translation are all tasks that used to require specialized skill, specialized software, or specialized labor, and are now accessible to people without any of those. The skill and resource barriers are lower in many domains.

AI compresses execution timelines by handling labor-intensive stages quickly, but it introduces new time costs. The generation itself is fast. Learning the tool, crafting prompts, reviewing outputs against your standards, revising or re-prompting when outputs do not meet your standards, and verifying accuracy when accuracy matters are real time costs. For some tasks, the new time costs are smaller than the time savings, and for others, they are larger. The honest answer depends on the specific task, the creator's skill level, and the standards being applied.

Effort shifts from execution to direction. Less time spent making, more time spent deciding what to make and evaluating what got made. Direction skills, like knowing what you want and recognizing quality and articulating standards, become more valuable. Execution skills can become less directly necessary for production while remaining essential for direction because you cannot direct what you cannot recognize.

Consider a writer working on a personal essay. AI can produce drafts in seconds, which compresses the time from idea to first draft. The new time costs are real, prompting the model effectively, reading what it produced, recognizing where the draft falls short of the writer's standards, and either revising heavily or just starting over. The writer is now spending more time in direction, on choices about voice and structure and what the essay is for, and less time in execution on the sentence-level labor of producing draft text. Whether that trade is worth it depends on the work, the writer's standards, and what kind of essay this is.

AI introduces new constraints that did not exist before. Access to AI resources. If your practice depends on a tool, you're depending on continued access to it. Once you have adapted your workflow to a tool, switching costs are real. The tool changing or going away has consequences beyond just losing access.

Learning curves. Getting good with AI tools takes real time and practice. In addition, the tools are changing quickly and require ongoing learning.

Audience pattern matching against AI outputs. The flood of generated content in many fields means audiences are pattern matching against AI outputs, whether you use AI or not. If your audience has started to associate certain stylistic markers with AI generation, those markers shape how your work is received, even if AI is not involved in making it. Distinguishing your work from the surrounding noise becomes a constraint that did not exist before, regardless of your own AI decisions.

Consider a graphic designer who has integrated an AI image tool into their iterative concepting process. The visible costs are access to the AI tool itself and the time spent prompting and reviewing. The hidden costs include the time invested in learning the tool, workflow changes, and dependency on a specific tool's continued availability.

The value lens describes what your work is for. The production lens describes what it costs to make. If the cost of producing baseline output drops towards zero, the differentiator becomes what the work is for, who it speaks to, and what makes it specific. Expression, connection, and distinctiveness become more legible as the production differentiator shrinks.

Next, we'll look at the 4D Framework to understand how AI is best used in your specific situation.
