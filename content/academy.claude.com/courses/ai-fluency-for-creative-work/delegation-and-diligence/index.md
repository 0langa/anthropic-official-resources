## Key takeaways[](https://academy.claude.com/courses/ai-fluency-for-creative-work/delegation-and-diligence)

- Problem Awareness comes first: be clear on the problem you are actually solving before reaching for a tool. Platform Awareness comes next: you cannot decide what to hand to a tool you do not understand. Then Task Delegation assigns the work, and Creation, Transparency, and Deployment Diligence help you delegate responsibly.
- Check finished work against your standards.
- Convergence and skill atrophy are the long-term pitfalls; reaching for AI when you do not know what you want is the immediate one.

Run one real project through the full outer loop and produce a one-page decision you can stand behind. The starting position is no AI; the analysis determines whether and how you opt in.

#### Part I: Name the project

In one paragraph: what the work is for, your standards for it, and which Value Lens dimensions carry the most weight.

#### Part II: Problem Awareness

Before choosing a tool, get clear on the problem itself. What are you actually trying to solve at this stage? What would a good outcome look like — and is this a problem where AI genuinely helps, or one better handled another way? Misreading the problem is the most common reason a Delegation goes wrong.

#### Part III: Platform Awareness

Reconsider the tool from your Production Lens audit: what can it do, and where could it help here? If you do not know it well enough to say, that is your answer: you are not ready to delegate to it yet.

#### Part IV: Task Delegation

Stage by stage, decide where AI fits and where it does not, then run each through the three Diligence checks (Creation, Transparency, Deployment).

## Transcript

Up to this point, the course has been building your analytical toolkit. The creative value lens, the production lens, and an articulated picture of your own practice with standards attached. This module is where that toolkit gets pointed at an actual decision. We will break Delegation and Diligence into their working parts, walk one designer's decision end-to-end, and explore common pitfalls so you can see the entire process before applying it to your own work.

The 4D Framework breaks Delegation into three subcomponents. Problem awareness is derived from the articulation work you just completed. You already know what your practice is, what your standards are, and what a given piece of work is for. Platform awareness is understanding the AI tools you're considering, what the tools actually do, what their outputs look like, what tendencies they have, what they're good at, and where their limits show up. Platform awareness comes before task delegation, because you cannot decide what to delegate to a tool until you understand the tool. A tool's tendencies and limits are part of its platform, and the analysis of those tendencies is part of Delegation.

It's important to remember that the AI you're using today will be the worst AI you'll ever use, and what the platforms are capable of shifts constantly. Task delegation follows from platform awareness combined with problem awareness. Given what you know about the work, and what you know about the tool, where does AI fit in your specific process and where does it not?

Diligence also has three sub-components. Creation diligence is about tool selection. Which systems fit your work, on what terms, with what capabilities, for what tasks. The Delegation analysis informs creation diligence, but the Diligence work itself is the careful choice and the careful use.

Transparency diligence is about disclosure. The audience, the client, the industry, the field, and the contract. Each carry expectations about what should be disclosed and to whom. Transparency diligence is the work of understanding your specific context and meeting what it calls for. Deployment diligence is about taking responsibility for the work that gets released. Verification against your standards, quality control on AI-influenced output, standing behind what you put into the world. The work the audience receives is your responsibility regardless of how it was made.

The Delegation analysis you're about to do begins with 'No AI' as the default position and works toward whether and how to opt in. Arriving at 'No AI' through this analysis is a legitimate outcome. Arriving at AI involvement through this analysis is also a legitimate outcome. The point is that the outcome is the result of analysis rather than a default.

Let's work through a real example. Consider Tom, a graphic designer working on a brand identity system for a regional arts organization. Problem awareness is already complete. Tom needs to create a visual identity, logo, color scheme, and tagline that resonates with artisanal creators. He researches specific AI tools to understand how it can help in his process. After thorough analysis of multiple tools' aesthetic tendencies, he decides that he wants to use AI to do initial concepting and keep the final outputs his own. Tom, after completing platform awareness, is now able to delegate specific tasks and asks AI to ideate concepts, and test multiple color palettes in his exploration stage.

Before Tom moves forward, he runs through the subcomponents of Diligence. He carefully reviews the tools he's selected to understand their specific terms and implications for any work he uploads. This is creation diligence. Then he connects with the organization to make sure they're comfortable with AI involvement in the design process. Transparency diligence asks what needs to be disclosed to meet expectations from the audience.

Deployment diligence is the last piece. Tom prompts his selected AI tools. He verifies the work, applying his aesthetic standards to make sure the final concept exploration produces real range, and the chosen direction reflects the community rather than the tool defaults. The Delegation-Diligence loop does not produce a single right answer. It produces a defensible decision the designer can stand behind.

Executing the Delegation-Diligence loop is nuanced, and there are a few pitfalls that creatives can encounter. The first is convergence. Your work starts looking like every other AI-assisted piece in your field, even when you think you're steering hard. An illustrator avoids the obvious "AI look," but still ends up in the same visual neighborhood as everyone else using the same model. So before you bring AI into a creative process, ask yourself where the distinctive part of your work actually gets made. For a brand designer, it might live in the concept. So AI-generated mood boards are low risk, but AI-generated final marks are not. For a documentary editor, it might live in the cut itself, frame by frame.

The second is skill atrophy and dependence. You direct, AI executes, and execution muscles you've built over years weaken. That matters because good direction depends on knowing execution from the inside. Know your tool's tendencies well enough to spot them in your output. Check finished work against your standards, not against what the tool produces easily. Find out what your client, publisher, or audience expects around disclosure, because the answer varies a lot by field.

If you're reaching for AI because you're unsure what you want to make, that's a signal to step back, not delegate. Choosing no AI for parts of your practice is a completely valid outcome of this thinking. So is heavy AI involvement. What matters is that you decided on purpose.

You now have all the tools you need to make intentional, ethical, and safe decisions about when and how to use AI in your work. Remember, you are in the driver's seat, and your every decision about when and where to use AI is yours to make.
