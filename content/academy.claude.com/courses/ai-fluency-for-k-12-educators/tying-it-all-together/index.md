## Exercises[](https://academy.claude.com/courses/ai-fluency-for-k-12-educators/tying-it-all-together)

### End-to-end 4D workflow

You've practiced each of the four Ds separately. Now put them together on a real task from your own work. The goal: a completed, usable output you own, and a record of how you got there.

*Part I: Choose your task* (in your own notes)

Pick a substantive, real task from your own work—due in the next week or two—that AI might genuinely help with. Before you start, note what "done" looks like, which parts AI can help with and which stay yours, and confirm you've stripped any student, staff, or personnel data and picked an appropriate tool.

*Part II: Run the full workflow*

Work through the task with AI, taking notes as you go:

1. What did you ask AI to do, and how did you frame it? Did you revise your prompt?
2. What did the output get right? What was wrong, thin, or missing?
3. What did you keep, change, or throw out—and why?
4. What did you add that only you could provide?
5. Would you put your name on the final output? Do you need to be transparent with anyone about how it was produced?

*Part III: Debrief* (in your own notes)

With a completed output in hand, reflect:

- How did each competency—Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence—play out? Where did you loop back or change course?
- Which Part II question did you write the most about, and which felt hardest? What does that tell you about where your instincts are strongest, and where you still have room to grow?

Bring your log to a colleague and debrief—not to compare outputs, but to share processes.

Let's consider an end-to-end task. Perhaps you're preparing a lesson for your students or a training for other teachers. In this lesson, we're going to use all four dimensions of the 4D Framework together on exactly that kind of task. This is where the pieces stop being separate skills you practice one at a time, and they start working as one system. Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence in sequence on something real.

Marcus is a teacher trainer supporting a cohort of first-year teachers heading into their second semester. He's been asked to put together a session on differentiated instruction—what it is, how to actually do it, and what it looks like when it's working. He's got two weeks and limited prep time. His educators teach secondary and high school, serving students with a variety of needs. The teachers are exhausted but motivated, which means they'll show up and they'll try, but they have zero patience for theory that doesn't translate into something that they can use on Monday morning. Marcus knows this because he was them not that long ago.

The 4Ds aren't a checklist he's going to move through once in order. They're more of a loop. He'll start with one, move to the next, realize something upstream needs adjusting, and circle back. That's normal. That's what fluency actually looks like when you're working on something real.

Before he opens any tool, Marcus has to figure out what he's actually handing off. Not everything in this task is the same kind of work, and AI is going to help with some of it a lot more than the rest. He thinks it through, and it sorts like this.

AI can handle a lot. It can help pull together current differentiation frameworks and distill what's actually useful from each. It can generate example activities that fit a secondary classroom. It can draft a session agenda with realistic timing. It can suggest ways to model differentiation inside of the session itself.

Marcus wants to keep a few things for himself. Specifically, the personal opening. He's planning to start with a story from his own first year, the week that he tried to teach the same lesson to 32 kids reading at eight different grade levels and watched it fall apart. AI also doesn't get to decide which frameworks make the final cut or which activities fit this specific group of teachers. That's judgment, and it stays with him.

This is Delegation. He's not asking whether AI could technically produce a full session plan. It could. He's asking which parts of the work get better when he brings AI in and which parts get weaker. The research and the scaffolding get faster. The credibility and the fit only happen if he stays in the chair.

Now he actually builds it. He needs to be specific about three things: how he wants the output to be shaped, how the AI should work through the problem, and what constraints actually matter.

He wants a 90-minute session outline on differentiated instruction, a mix of direct instruction, modeling, and hands-on practice. Something that ends with every person in the room holding a concrete plan for one class one week. It should work by starting with two or three current frameworks that actually get used in secondary classrooms—not ones that only live in textbooks. For each one, it should show what it looks like in practice with specific examples. Then it should build towards a practice block where teachers adapt one framework to a lesson they're already planning to teach.

For the constraints that actually matter: these are first-year teachers in high-need schools. No activity that requires prep time they don't have, no materials that they have to buy or print in color. Everything has to work in a class with desks, a whiteboard, and whatever they can carry in their bag.

Here's what this looks like. Marcus doesn't just type "help me plan a PD on differentiation." He gives it the audience, the goal, the format, and the constraints upfront. He's not testing whether the tool can guess what he needs. He's telling it.

He rewrites the two activities so they run on a hypothetical roster instead of real student data, which means people can actually do them in the room. He tightens the modeling block to leave more time for practice, because he knows from experience that the practice block is where the session either lands or doesn't.

This is the loop. Describe, review, describe again. The first draft was raw material, not a finished product. It got him 70% of the way in 15 minutes, and the remaining 30% is exactly the part that needs his judgment anyway.

Last piece. The draft is in good shape. Now it's about how he finishes it and what he does with it once it's done.

He weaves in his own material. The opening story is his, in his words. The framing around each framework reflects what he's actually seen work and not work. The AI built the scaffold. He's the one making it into a session this group will remember.
