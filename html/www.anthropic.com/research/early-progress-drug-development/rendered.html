    Early progress on accelerating drug development with Claude
View more

Anthropic’s life sciences team is working to accelerate curing disease. Over the past several months, our scientists have engaged in deep research partnerships with six organizations developing new medicines: BigHat Biosciences, Bristol Myers Squibb, Divergence Labs, Enveda, Generate Biomedicines, and The Column Group. We've also begun drug discovery efforts of our own. In doing so, we've learned a lot about how Claude can support drug development, and how we’ll need to improve Claude further to realize AI's potential in medicine. We plan to scale this work up substantially from here.

There are many opportunities to speed up drug development, even beyond the most commonly discussed applications of AI, like proposing initial designs for new molecules. Developing a drug involves many varied tasks: doing early research to identify a target for a drug and the right type of drug to engage it, designing a molecule with the right properties, optimizing it to be manufactured reliably at scale, drafting the regulatory filings needed to run clinical trials, designing and administering those trials, and much more.

Our strategy to realize AI’s medical benefits is based on having AI increasingly involved in all of these tasks. There is no single problem to solve; the bottlenecks are distributed all throughout the drug development process. The best way to identify and work through these bottlenecks is to get deeply involved in real drug programs, across many different disease areas and stages of development.

So, over the past several months, we’ve partnered with six organizations to do so. We’ve also started drug discovery efforts of our own, including programs for rare childhood forms of muscular dystrophy, where Claude handles much of the scientific and operational work.

This work serves our goals in three ways. First, it lets us better understand the strengths and limitations of our models in relation to a much wider range of drug development tasks (we’ve already prioritized specific improvements to our models based on what we’ve learned so far). Second, these programs can serve as case studies for the wider life sciences community. If Claude is to significantly increase the rate of new cures, we need to work with many more biotechnology firms and integrate Claude more deeply into their development processes. Third, these projects should, if their positive trajectories continue, have direct impact themselves. We’re optimistic that some may result in effective new medicines for the diseases they target, including food allergies, rare childhood diseases, and several forms of cancer.

In this post, we provide an update on our progress with our partners, along with technical reports that describe our thought process, methods, and early results in more detail. We then briefly outline where our work is headed next.

Case studies

Generate Biomedicines: modality engineering. Generate Biomedicines has been working on increasing the usefulness of a cancer treatment known as CAR T-cell therapy. This therapy has historically helped treat blood cell cancers; Generate aims to reengineer it so that it can treat solid tumor cancers too. To do this, they’re aiming to shorten one part of the therapy’s genetic instructions, which would create room for additional instructions suited to other kinds of cancer. Six Generate scientists used Claude Code to propose new designs for this part. One design was about 70% shorter than the standard version, and in lab tests it was more effective: it allowed immune cells to make roughly 10 times as many receptors (the proteins they use to spot cancer cells), it killed tumor cells more efficiently, and the immune cells remained active for longer. Read more, and see Generate’s technical report.

Divergence Labs: hit generation. Divergence Labs is working on a novel way to treat food allergies. Food allergies are set off by a reaction from a molecule called IgE, some of which is attached to our immune cells and some of which floats freely in our blood. Divergence Labs is developing drugs that would detach the IgE from our immune cells entirely, rather than solely neutralizing the IgE circulating in our blood, as standard medications do. This could provide a much more effective treatment, but it requires a precise design, with two “arms”: one that would bind tightly to IgE, and another that would help prise IgE from the immune cell. Claude converted Divergence Labs’ goals into a detailed design plan, and then, largely autonomously, generated roughly 35,000 antibody designs in about a month to be narrowed down and tested. In those early tests, Divergence found “hits” for both arms: three designs that could bind to IgE, and 20 that could dislodge it. Three of those 20 appeared to dislodge IgE more than the standard medication. Its next step is to build a drug that combines the two arms. Read more, and see Divergence’s technical report.

BigHat Biosciences: lead optimization. BigHat Biosciences pairs AI with an automated lab to improve potential drugs (or “leads”), which must bind their target tightly, remain stable through manufacturing and storage, and avoid clumping together (among other criteria). BigHat’s scientists and an Anthropic researcher worked with Claude to improve these three properties in two leads, both antibody fragments that could become parts of future drugs: one designed to target cancer cells, and one designed to keep a drug in the body longer. Claude, connected to BigHat’s lab data system, Reccy, proposed changes to each molecule, and BigHat’s lab made and tested about 1,010 variants in about 10 weeks. Partway through, the team suspected the standard heat-stability and clumping metrics were misleading. Claude and the researcher reanalyzed the raw data, then proposed different metrics and a protocol change to test them. BigHat’s scientists refined and ran it, and the main predictions held. Compared with the originals, the best versions bound more than 10x more tightly, the cancer-targeting lead stayed in solution 18°C higher, and the lead for longer-lasting drugs clumped more than 100x more slowly. Read more, and see BigHat’s technical report.

Enveda: manufacturing and filings. To conduct clinical trials, companies need records showing that each batch of a new drug has been made to strict quality standards, and to submit an application to regulators, among other requirements. At Enveda, manufacturing chemists spent up to 75% of their time on paperwork. To ease the load, Enveda built a system that connects all of its manufacturing documents to Claude, then lets Claude flag any possible issues. Over tests on 1,061 pages of documentation, Claude caught all 11 issues that human reviewers had spotted—and flagged seven they’d missed. In the process, this method reduced human review time by 89%, from over 25 hours to less than three. Separately, Claude wrote the first drafts of two important regulatory documents. One took four hours, compared to about three months by hand. For the other, Enveda’s standard human review process found one minor error. Read more, and see Enveda’s technical report.

The Column Group (TCG): company creation. Because drug discovery can be slow, expensive, and prone to failure, investors in biotechnology companies have favored safer, previously validated ideas over riskier new ones, even when the latter could help more patients. We’ve been working with TCG to see whether Claude can change this by cutting the time and cost of drug discovery so that investors can confidently support more ambitious ideas. Specifically, TCG is establishing Pleiades, a new company that will use Claude to run many more—and more complex—drug programs than is usual for a small team. This could be a model for how Claude can reduce both the scientific and the operational and administrative overhead required to run a biotechnology company. If this works, this model could be replicated across the industry and direct investment toward harder problems in medicine. Pleiades is currently developing new tools and workflows for Claude; its very first Claude skill, which helps Claude assess whether drug targets are worth pursuing, has been evaluated on 45 target-disease pairs. Read more.

More recently, we’ve also begun working with Bristol Myers Squibb (BMS) to find and address bottlenecks in clinical trial planning. BMS is using Claude to assess plans of an early-stage drug in clinical trials, and to estimate the likelihood of those trials succeeding. Together, we plan to expand this work in 2027 to understand its potential for increasing the probability of success in early development.

“We use Claude at the clinical trial stage to analyze proprietary data, create a clinical study plan, and weigh the study’s chance of success. From a single prompt, Claude generated a robust initial analysis that helped inform our assessment of different development options, which would otherwise take days to complete. We did all of this while ensuring the security of our highly sensitive data. We look forward to expanding this work across our pipeline to support portfolio-level decisions.”

Michael Pourdehnad, MD, Senior Vice President, Head of Early Clinical Development, Hematology, Oncology and Cell Therapy.

In addition to our work with these partners, we’ve begun to run some research programs ourselves. We’ve acquired Mithril Medicine, a one-person company that has been developing treatments for two rare forms of muscular dystrophy. Its founder, Cody Tranbarger, used Claude to organize contracts, logistics, and experiments with 11 external labs, and delegated many other aspects of Mithril’s operations to Claude. As a result, Mithril took a possible treatment for one form of muscular dystrophy from an idea to promising results in mice in less than 18 months. Now at Anthropic, Cody is continuing this research, and plans to start several new programs. If his drug candidates continue to hold up, we expect a drug to be ready to start clinical trials in humans as soon as late 2028. Read more and see our technical report.

Extending our work in the life sciences

All of this research is still at an early stage. In particular, so far it is mostly preclinical—that is, for the most part, it isn’t yet at the stage where the drugs are being tested in humans. We plan to continue extending Claude’s role into clinical trials as these drug programs advance, and as we partner with more organizations whose drug development programs are already at the clinical stage.

This research is only one part of our efforts to accelerate drug discovery. Ultimately, we want to share our models with as many drug development researchers as possible. To that end, we’ve rolled out Claude Science, our AI workbench for researchers, and we’re providing Claude credits for life scientists. We’ve also created the Life Sciences Verification Program to allow trusted organizations to use our most powerful models with more permissive versions of our safeguards, which are necessary to prevent misuse but which can impede legitimate biological research. We’ve also recently launched an open call for guest investigators in the life sciences; we’re seeking proposals from research groups who want to use Claude to investigate key questions in drug discovery and other areas.

We’re looking forward to working with many more organizations as we expand our life sciences research. If you work at an organization focused on drug discovery and are interested in collaborating with us, you can get in touch below.

Generate Biomedicines

Generate:Biomedicines: Redesigning a component of CAR T-cell therapy

The Generate:Biomedicines team shares how they worked with Claude to redesign a component of CAR T-cell therapy to make it more compact, freeing up space for new functions that could help treat cancer.

Read Generate’s technical report and get in touch (info@generatebiomedicines.com).

Generate:Biomedicines designs protein drug candidates using machine learning and frontier biohardware. Over the past eight months, a six-person team of Generate scientists redesigned a stubborn component of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, a type of cancer therapy, with help from Claude.

CAR T-cell therapy uses genetically engineered versions of patients’ own immune cells to attack cancer cells. The part the Generate scientists redesigned is called the signaling domain—the section of the engineered receptor that tells the immune cell to attack. Making it shorter frees up space for other functions in the CAR’s genetic instructions, such as attacking several cancer markers at once or adding proteins that help the T-cell survive inside a tumor.

Working with Claude and other AI tools, Generate’s scientists built and directed a system of AI agents to design signaling domains that are much more compact than a standard version used in several approved therapies. The best design was about 70% shorter and no less effective. In laboratory tests, one of the top designs put roughly 10 times as much receptor on the cell surface—which may make a T-cell better able to recognize and attack cancer cells—and the lead designs killed tumor cells more efficiently than the standard version.

  • Read Generate:Biomedicine’s technical report, “Refactored intracellular domains enhance CAR expression and function.”

Why improving CAR T-cell therapy is a challenge

CAR T-cell therapy has transformed the treatment of several blood cancers, but the drugs themselves are cumbersome to manufacture. Today, a patient’s T-cells are removed, engineered in a factory over several weeks, and infused back into the patient. A major goal for the field is to do that work inside the body: delivering the genetic instructions for building the receptor directly to the patient’s T-cells. This could be a faster and cheaper way to deliver the therapy and could expand what CAR T-cell therapy can do. In particular, it could make the therapy better suited to treating autoimmune conditions, as well as early-stage solid tumor cancers, against which it has so far been less successful.

But delivering genetic instructions into a living cell means working within tight constraints. Space is at a premium: the instructions are typically delivered either by a viral vector or a lipid nanoparticle carrying mRNA, both of which can only hold a limited amount of genetic material. It would be helpful to give the CAR more functions, or to bundle in protective proteins to help the T-cell survive inside a tumor. The more compact each part of the receptor is, the more room is left in the delivery vehicle for these extra functions.

One way of boosting a CAR’s functionality would be to improve the delivery system—for example, by designing a capsule that can carry more genetic instructions. To use a software analogy, it would be like making a larger flash drive that can carry more information. But another way would be to make the individual parts of the CAR shorter—like writing software more efficiently so more programs fit on a given drive. Just as a programmer refactors a codebase to make it more compact and efficient without changing what it does, Generate wanted to refactor the CAR to make it shorter but no less effective.

The team had already redesigned the part of the receptor that recognizes a marker on the surface of the cancer cell, but the signaling domain was a harder problem. Unlike the part that recognizes the marker, the signaling domain has no fixed three-dimensional shape to work from, and the way it does its job is not completely known. That made it a compelling challenge for Generate’s scientists: could they create and direct a system of reasoning agents to help redesign a protein whose structure and function were only partly understood? Would those agents be able to help the team explore novel designs that push the field’s understanding forward?

Building the agentic system

In February 2026, using Claude Code, the team built a system of AI agents running Claude and other models. Generate’s scientists directed the agents’ work through this system, giving them experimental lab results, prompting them based on specific hypotheses around CAR biology, providing feedback, and creatively exploring the design space together.

The agents then generated a set of proposals. Each presented a specific design for a shorter signaling domain, with the agents’ reasoning about what to cut, what to keep, and why the redesigned domain should still work effectively. The agents’ reasoning drew from published research, the scientists’ own insights, and Generate’s experimental data. Through rounds of review and steering, the team selected 186 designs for the first round of lab testing.

To test the designs, Generate’s scientists wrote the genetic instructions for each redesigned receptor, introduced those instructions into cells in the lab, and observed how the cells behaved. The first round of scientific hypothesis generation and experimentation took two weeks, from proposal selection to initial results.

Based on these experiments, the team identified several shortened signaling domains that worked better than the standard design and were less likely to fire when the target marker is absent, which can wear T-cells out over time. Generate’s scientists fed those results back into the system to direct the search toward a few strong leads.

What the team found

One of the strongest candidates, designed by Generate’s scientists working with Claude, had a signaling domain about 70% shorter than the standard version. When tested in human T-cells in the lab, it led to roughly 10 times as many copies of the receptor on the cells’ surface. Receptors are how a T-cell recognizes its target, so producing more receptors per molecule of drug delivered may improve the T-cell’s ability to find its target and work as intended.

The lead candidates killed tumor cells more efficiently than the standard version, and they released more of a key chemical signal that T-cells use to rally the rest of the immune system. Generate believes both improvements were driven in part by the higher number of receptors on each cell—which also means less mRNA is needed to make a working CAR-T cell—and in part by increased potency.

That surplus of receptors could also make new kinds of CAR T-cell therapy possible. For instance, instead of carrying many copies of a single receptor, a T-cell might carry several different types of receptor, each aimed at a different marker, with enough of each type to remain effective. That could be useful against solid tumors, in which different cells don’t always share the same markers. Separately, because receptors gradually wear away, having more of them could help each cell remain effective longer.

These are early laboratory results—Generate has not yet tested that the designs are safe or effective in patients. Read more about the methods and findings in Generate’s preprint.

Rethinking how the team works with Claude

Over the course of the project, the way the team worked with Claude changed too. At first, one of Generate’s wet lab biologists used Claude Code to build software tools for colleagues who didn’t code. Among these tools was a customized electronic lab notebook system—essentially, a digital record of experiments—that better served the team’s needs than existing off-the-shelf options. Within a few weeks, the rest of the biologists were building tools with Claude Code themselves.

A few months later, the whole team was working directly in Claude Code—it had become a “homepage” of sorts for the team, and was connected to Generate’s data, tools, and custom applications. They’d become accustomed to working with the agents to retrieve and compare experimental data, offer hypotheses about what might have gone wrong in a failed experiment, and suggest experiments to try next. The agents had become part of the team.

What’s next

The work is still at an early stage, and the next step is to test how the designs perform in more complex preclinical models, such as mice. Generate and Anthropic are now working together on the target and indication strategy for applying the redesigned CAR to solid tumors. They plan to work with Claude to reason across broad datasets—both public ones and Generate’s own—to select the optimal combination of tumor markers to target to effectively kill cancer cells. They hope this work will result in a lead drug candidate for solid-tumor cancer in the near future.

Divergence Labs

Divergence Labs: Speeding up antibody design for food allergies

The team at Divergence Labs describes how they worked with Claude to design early antibody candidates for a new food allergy drug.

Read Divergence’s technical report and get in touch (careers@divergencelabs.com).

Divergence Labs is a biotechnology company founded in 2025 by an interdisciplinary team of drug hunters, computer scientists, biologists, and hardware engineers. It recently identified an opportunity to engineer better medicines for food allergies, which affect around 33 million people in the US alone.

Food allergies are caused by an abnormal immune response in which the body mistakenly identifies proteins in foods such as peanuts or tree nuts as harmful. This has the potential to cause life-threatening immune reactions. In 2024, Xolair (omalizumab) became the first FDA-approved medication to reduce allergic reactions to multiple foods. Although this is a major advance, over half of patients on Xolair still cannot consume more than a 2,000 mg dose at a time, of two or more foods they’re allergic to (roughly eight peanuts). Xolair reduces the risk of a reaction after accidental exposure, but patients must still avoid their trigger foods and carry an EpiPen in case of a larger exposure. These limitations are related to the molecular design of Xolair, opening up an opportunity to design a better medicine from scratch.

Below, Divergence shares how its researchers worked with Claude to design early antibody candidates that could serve as the building blocks for a new, more effective type of food allergy drug. (Read the technical report for more details.) Claude did much of the work autonomously, and the team had lab results within weeks, compared with up to nine months in previous projects. The results suggest that AI could help researchers design more complex drugs and free up resources to work on more of them at once.

  • Read Divergence Labs’ technical report, “Towards a best-in-class IgE disruptor for food allergy with Claude-enabled antibody design.”

Going beyond existing therapeutic mechanisms

Xolair is an antibody that neutralizes immunoglobulin E (IgE), the molecule that sets off the immune system’s reaction to food and environmental allergens. There are two predominant forms of IgE: free and bound. Free IgE circulates in the blood; bound IgE is already attached to immune cells. Xolair was designed to neutralize free IgE (i.e., it is an “inhibitor”). It can also dislodge bound IgE, but in patients this only occurs at concentrations far higher than one could practically receive by injection. Yet bound IgE is the form that sets off the allergic reaction: when a person comes into contact with an allergen, the IgE molecules on the surface of an immune cell cluster together, signaling the cell to respond. A more effective medicine would therefore need to both neutralize free IgE and disrupt bound IgE, making it both an inhibitor and a “disruptor.”

Disrupting bound IgE is unusually difficult, however. A drug has to attach at exactly the right spot to knock an IgE molecule off the immune cell it’s anchored to. What’s more, traditional antibodies have two arms, so a single antibody can grab two bound IgE molecules at once and pull them together. This causes the same clustering that sets off an allergic reaction—meaning an antibody-based drug could trigger the exact immune response it’s meant to prevent. Removing bound IgE safely and effectively therefore requires precise control over a drug’s shape and how it binds, which is hard to achieve with conventional methods of antibody discovery.

Finding a drug candidate requires improving many properties at once, including how well it works, how stable it is, and how easily it can be manufactured. Claude turned the research team’s goal of disrupting bound IgE into a detailed design plan consisting of discrete phases, then carried out most of that plan autonomously, pausing after each phase so Divergence’s researchers could review the results and decide on next steps.

Claude started by analyzing every known antibody that targets IgE, and pinpointed the structural features that allow some of them to dislodge bound IgE. Divergence’s research team chose a design with two different arms: an “anchor arm” to grip the IgE molecule, and a “disruptor arm” to break its hold on the immune cell. Because both arms attach to the same IgE molecule, this design is also intended to avoid the clustering problem described above. Each arm would be designed separately, then joined into a single antibody. Claude helped the team identify where on the IgE molecule each arm should attach, in order to accurately combine the two.

Claude then split the design work into two projects, one for each arm. For the disruptor arm, it set out to improve on a promising design from an earlier study. For the anchor arm, Claude had to create a design from scratch. The new anchor arm design also had to be compatible with the disruptor arm design, so that both could attach to the same IgE molecule at once.

For both projects, Divergence provided Claude with in-house molecular design tools, along with existing protein modeling methods that the team had adapted for this problem with Claude’s support. One adapted method was particularly successful at increasing the functional activity of the disruptor arm. Divergence’s design model normally builds a protein from scratch, but the modified version could start from an existing one. This made it possible to make small, targeted changes to the earlier design and improve it quickly.

In total, Claude generated about 35,000 designs over 22 separate stretches of autonomous work. This took around 2,750 hours of computing time spread across many Nvidia H100 GPUs. Claude then used biomolecular structure prediction models to predict how well each design would attach to IgE, and kept only the highest scoring ones—no more than 96 per arm—to make and test in the lab. Going from the initial idea to lab results took roughly three weeks for the disruptor arm and four and a half weeks for the anchor arm. Previously, comparable work had taken Divergence up to nine months.

Experimental results

The Divergence team then tested the disruptor arm designs in the lab. They used a technique called biolayer interferometry (BLI), which uses light to track molecules attaching to and detaching from one another in real time. This allowed them to see whether each design could knock IgE off the receptor that binds it to an immune cell.

In the first round of testing, 20 of the 91 designs worked as intended, measurably dislodging bound IgE within one to two minutes. In further testing, three of these appeared to outperform Xolair at concentrations patients could realistically receive. The 20 working designs were also quite different from one another, differing at as many as 8 positions in their protein sequences from the reference. This has given the team a varied set of starting points to refine into potential therapies.

The team also tested 57 of the anchor arm designs, and confirmed that three of them (around 5%) attached to IgE. The best of the three gripped it very tightly, with a binding affinity comparable to many approved antibody drugs.

What’s next for the team

Divergence set out to design a drug that does what Xolair can’t: dislodge the bound IgE that sets off an allergic reaction, at doses patients could realistically receive. Working with Claude, the team now has early versions of the two parts such a drug would need. In lab tests, the best disruptor arms appeared to dislodge bound IgE more effectively than Xolair, and the best anchor arms gripped IgE tightly.

Some of the problems Claude solved were more conceptual. For example, computer models can’t directly measure whether an antibody will knock IgE off its receptor, so Claude chose a stand-in these models could measure: the shape of the IgE molecule, which is “open” when attached to a receptor and “closed” when a known disruptor has attached to it. After confirming that the models reproduced both shapes correctly, Claude discarded any design that didn’t hold IgE closed. It then analyzed the results to inform later phases of the project. These are the types of time-consuming tasks that limit the pace of protein engineering today. If researchers can confidently hand this work to Claude, they can begin to pursue several projects in parallel.

There’s still room for improvement. When given a design objective, Claude was good at setting up a round of design work, including deciding what to measure and what to filter for. In certain cases, however, the team found that Claude was less pragmatic and contextually grounded than a human operator in deciding between further investigating a result and moving on. In one phase, for example, the goal was to explore if a particular strategy could redesign one of the loops the disruptor arm uses to grip IgE while preserving the design’s function. The first set of results was definitive, but Claude proposed five follow-up design runs, each with slightly different settings that were unlikely to change the key conclusion. In contrast, the human operator suggested a focus on a previously unexplored, alternative strategy. Choosing the experiments that are most worth running remains a judgment call that researchers, at the moment, seem to be better positioned to make.

The results described here are a long way from a new medicine. Finding promising initial designs, known as hits, is only the first of many steps in drug development. Additional hit optimization work (e.g., avidity-based constraints, developability) is still required.

As a next step, the Divergence team plans to use computer modeling to determine which anchor and disruptor arms are most compatible, then build and test complete two-armed antibodies. The most promising of these two-armed combinations will be tested on the immune cells involved in allergic reactions, to show whether they can remove bound IgE without triggering the immune response they’re meant to prevent.

Divergence expects these methods to be useful well beyond food allergies. Indeed, they might be applicable to any condition that requires a drug to do something more complex than attach to its target. But for now, the team’s focus remains on the 33 million people in the US with food allergies, and on creating a treatment that could one day mean they no longer need to avoid trigger foods or carry an EpiPen.

BigHat Biosciences

BigHat Biosciences: Optimizing antibody drug candidates with Claude

BigHat Biosciences shares how an Anthropic researcher working with Claude improved two antibodies on binding, stability, and aggregation in about 10 weeks—and improved on how those properties were being measured.

Read BigHat’s technical report and get in touch (partnering@bighatbio.com).

BigHat Biosciences is a clinical-stage biotechnology company that designs and develops next-generation protein therapeutics: antibody-based drugs engineered to function in ways the body’s own antibodies can’t. BigHat pairs AI models with an automated lab: the models propose new molecules, and the lab makes and tests thousands of them a week. The results of every round of experiments are fed back to the models, so that each new set of designs is informed by the last.

Designing an antibody-based drug is a balancing act. An antibody works by binding to a specific target in the body, such as a protein on the surface of a cancer cell. But the drug also has to be sturdy enough to keep its shape through manufacturing, shipping, and storage, which scientists predict by measuring the drug’s “thermostability,” the temperature to which it can be heated before it unfolds. And the drug’s molecules have to avoid sticking to one another and forming clumps, a process known as aggregation. Changes that help with one of these properties often make another worse. For example, reshaping the part of the antibody that grips the target can improve binding, but it can also loosen the structure that keeps the molecule folded, reducing its thermostability.

Managing these trade-offs is the work of an antibody engineer. In a process called lead optimization, the engineer takes an antibody that already binds to its target and refines it to make a better drug. BigHat and Anthropic wanted to find out whether a scientist working closely with Claude could do this work. Could an experienced researcher, paired with Claude, improve an antibody’s binding, thermostability, and resistance to clumping, all at once?

To find out, the BigHat team chose two existing antibodies as starting points. For each one, a researcher at Anthropic worked with Claude to design a batch of 176 slightly altered versions, known as variants. BigHat’s lab then made and tested each variant, and the researcher and Claude used those results to design the next batch. After three rounds of this process for one antibody and four for the other, each had yielded a variant that outperformed the original on all three properties: it gripped its target more tightly, held together at higher temperatures, and resisted clumping better.

Claude’s contribution to this project went beyond designing antibodies. Partway through, the lab’s scores for stability and clumping proved erratic, and BigHat’s quality checks flagged many of them as unreliable. The researcher suspected the problem was in how the scores were being calculated. He and Claude went back to the instrument’s raw readings, where Claude worked out metrics better suited to these molecules and proposed a change to the lab’s protocol to check them. Once BigHat’s results bore them out, the teams used the revised scores to choose their final molecules. These results suggest that AI can speed up the search for answers, and also help scientists ask better questions.

  • Read BigHat Bioscience’s pre-publication research paper, “Reasoning in the loop: mechanism and measurement in antibody engineering.”

Two molecules

For its pilot projects, BigHat chose two starting molecules, each a small antibody fragment that could become a building block in a future drug. The first, an anti-albumin VHH, is one of the smallest antibody fragments found in nature. It binds to albumin, the most abundant protein in human blood; attaching an albumin-binding piece to a drug is a common way of keeping that drug in the body for longer. The second, an anti-mesothelin scFv, is a stripped-down, lab-made antibody that targets mesothelin, a protein that is rare in healthy adults but common on the surface of many cancer cells. An antibody that sticks to mesothelin can deliver a cancer drug to a tumor or draw the body’s immune cells to the tumor to attack it.

BigHat set the same goals for each pilot. The new molecule had to reach a target level of binding about 10 times tighter than the original molecule’s, survive being heated to between 65 and 70°C without unfolding or clumping, and still be producible in BigHat’s lab. A molecule that balances binding, stability, and clumping well enough to be manufactured as a drug is said to be “developable.”

The teams measured stability and clumping with a single heating test, in which an instrument heats each sample and records two signals: the color of the light given off by the protein itself, which shifts as the molecule unfolds, and the light scattered by the sample, which increases as molecules clump together. The instrument’s software then reduces each test to two numbers: an estimated melting temperature (the temperature at which half of the molecules have unfolded), taken from the first signal, and the temperature at which it starts to clump, taken from the second. The teams relied on these two numbers to decide which variants to move into the next round.

A different way of working

In many labs, improvements to a molecule are found largely by trial and error. One method is to try each of the 20 amino acids—the building blocks of proteins—at a single position in the antibody and see which version scores best across the three properties. Another method, known as directed evolution, is to make random changes to the molecule, keep the versions that perform best, combine the helpful changes from different versions into new ones, and repeat.

More recently, teams have trained specialized AI models to help with this. Some propose new amino acid sequences, and others predict how a sequence will score. Some labs now connect these models directly to their equipment. But in these setups, researchers decide at the outset which tests to run and what scores qualify as success. The model’s job is to find molecules that score well, not to question whether the tests are measuring the right thing or to suggest running them in a different way.

These pilots worked differently. BigHat runs its lab through a custom-built system called Reccy, which keeps a centralized record of every molecule, physical sample, instrument run, and result. Reccy has a built-in connector for AI agents, which Anthropic used to link it to Claude Code. This gave Claude instant access to any of the data it might need during the experiments.

A researcher at Anthropic worked with Claude to design each round of variants. Claude proposed the changes itself, without relying on specialized tools. It chose them by reasoning about how each change would affect the molecule’s structure and physical behavior, and by studying the lab’s results from earlier rounds. When it needed a deeper analysis, it built simple statistical models from the results. The researcher asked Claude to explain its reasoning for each proposed change, and the two of them decided which variants to send to the lab. BigHat’s lab then made and tested the molecules, and the results were recorded in Reccy, where Claude could read them.

Improving an antibody is a highly iterative, time-consuming process. Antibodies are usually made by putting DNA instructions into living cells and growing them until they have produced enough protein, which adds roughly a week to every round. To eliminate this bottleneck, BigHat’s lab uses an approach called cell-free protein synthesis. It takes the protein-making machinery out of cells and supplies it directly with the DNA instructions for an antibody, so there is no waiting for cells to grow and no need to break them open to recover the protein. This lets the lab make thousands of antibodies a week and measure several properties for each. Because the lab runs dozens of programs in parallel, these pilots used only a small part of that capacity.

In each round, the lab made 176 variants and measured how tightly each one bound its target, how it behaved when heated, and other properties, including its purity and how much was produced. Results typically came back in nine to 10 days: seven to produce the DNA for each variant, and the remainder to make and test the antibodies. Over about 10 weeks and seven rounds, the two pilots tested about 1,010 unique variants across 1,232 samples.

Claude quickly recognized that both starting molecules closely resembled antibodies already described in published patents. Comparing them with the published antibodies allowed Claude to suggest changes that were likely to improve binding. For the scFv, the cancer-targeting molecule, Claude identified a single amino acid change that it expected would tighten binding, a hypothesis which was confirmed in the lab. But binding was only one of the goals: Claude still had to improve stability and reduce clumping by changing other parts of each molecule without losing the gain in binding.

Working with Claude also allowed the teams to explore bolder designs. Antibody engineers typically change only one or two amino acids at a time, because the combined effect of many changes is hard to predict. Here, the teams were able to go much further, because Claude proposed precise changes and reasoned about which to combine and which to test separately. About 150 of the variants they tested differed from the starting molecule by 10 to 13 amino acids. In one round of the pilot for the VHH, the albumin-binding molecule, 65 variants had 10 or more changes, and every one of them still bound its target.

The teams also tested more than 200 variants with just one change each, to learn which positions in the molecule were most important for binding and which could be changed freely. And they ran small controlled experiments to test specific ideas about the best candidates. The two final molecules differ from their starting points by eight (for the VHH) and six (for the scFv) amino acids.

From antibody design to general scientific reasoning

Stability and clumping proved much harder to improve than binding. By the second round of results, Claude could predict binding reasonably well from one round to the next. It struggled to do the same for stability and clumping, especially on the VHH, where the scores didn’t seem to follow any pattern.

BigHat's scientists had already cautioned that these scores needed care, and the Anthropic researcher running the pilots suspected the same thing: that the problem lay not in the molecules but in how the instrument’s readings were being turned into scores. Because Reccy stores each instrument’s raw readings alongside the summary numbers, he and Claude could go back to the raw data and investigate.

The raw data for each sample consists of two curves, one for each signal, which show how the signal changes as the sample is heated. When Claude examined them, it found that the two could not be read separately: unfolding and clumping affect each other, so the curves have to be analyzed together. Read one at a time, the two summary numbers were misleading.

The scFv’s variants began to clump as soon as they began to unfold, and the clumps fell out of solution before unfolding was complete. The number being used as the “melting temperature” was really recording that collapse of the solution.

The numbers for the VHH variants were misleading in a different way. Most VHH variants began clumping at around 40°C, long before they unfolded. This could distort the unfolding signal, and some reported melting temperatures were more than 10°C too low. BigHat’s software had flagged more than half of these samples as unreliable, which was correct, but that left most of the molecules without a usable number. And because those variants all started clumping at about the same temperature, the clumping score could not tell good variants from bad ones. The relevant measure was how quickly they clumped. Telling the two processes apart matters, because a molecule that unfolds too easily is usually repaired by strengthening its interior, whereas one that clumps is usually repaired by changing its surface. A score that confuses the two will direct the designer to the wrong part of the molecule.

Claude proposed different scores that better matched how each molecule actually behaved. For the VHH, it proposed measuring clumping by how fast it happened, not by the temperature at which it started. For the scFv, it proposed measuring the temperature at which the sample collapsed (unfolding and clumping together), adjusted for how concentrated the sample was. To arrive at these recommendations, Claude ran an autonomous research session. It tested many possible explanations of how the molecules behaved as they heated up, generated the curve each explanation would produce, and kept those that best matched the real data.

To check that these scores reflected real properties of the molecules, the researcher and Claude proposed a change to the lab’s testing protocol. The standard protocol tests each sample under one heating rate and one concentration. Instead, the teams tested one set of samples at two concentrations and another at two heating rates. A true melting temperature would stay the same under all of these conditions, but a number that was in fact tracking clumping would change, because clumping depends on how concentrated the sample is and how long it spends at each temperature. Because BigHat’s lab is automated, the new conditions were incorporated into the very next round of tests.

Before the results came in, Claude predicted which numbers would hold steady and which would change, and its main predictions proved correct. The VHH’s melting temperature, read from the full curve, barely changed, which was indicative of a true melting temperature. The temperature at which the scFv collapsed shifted by two to five degrees, as would be expected if the instrument were really detecting clumping.

The revised scores made a practical difference. On the VHH, the instrument's clumping score could not separate molecules. No design in four rounds received a passing value that also cleared quality checks, including the final molecule, whose scattering stayed near baseline. With the revised scores, the teams could better distinguish design variants, and the final VHH clumped far more slowly than the one they started with.

Results and next steps

Each pilot produced a candidate that improved on its starting molecule in all three properties. Both reached the level of binding that BigHat had set as its goal. Compared with their starting molecules, they bound six to nine times more tightly, held together at temperatures 8 to 18°C higher, and resisted clumping better. Both could still be made in pure form and in sufficient quantity. These two were chosen for their balance, from a wider set of improved variants. The tightest binders in both pilots improved more than tenfold, about half of the final cancer-targeting variants bound too tightly for the assay to resolve, and the most stable cancer-targeting variants stayed in solution more than 20°C higher than the starting molecule. The work took about 10 weeks: one researcher and Claude designed the molecules, and BigHat’s team advised on the process and made and tested the designs.

The stability results in particular stood out. Small antibody fragments like the two in this pilot often begin to unfold somewhere between 50 and 65°C, and the industry generally wants a molecule to remain stable up to about 65°C before deeming it developable. The starting VHH melted at about 62°C, and the starting scFv collapsed at about 56°C. The final VHH melted at about 71°C and showed no clear sign of clumping before the instrument reached 85°C, its upper limit; the final scFv collapsed from solution at about 74°C.

But perhaps what stands out most is how the work was done: by a general-purpose AI model, working with a team of researchers, who jointly improved not only the molecules but the experimental process itself. The researchers supplied the suspicion that the scores were wrong and decided which hypotheses to test. Claude read every raw curve, modeled and compared possible explanations, and designed the experiments to validate them, all in a matter of days. Together, they questioned whether the scores were measuring the right thing—a question an AI model designed only to hit a fixed set of metrics is not built to ask.

The teams now plan to try out this way of working on more therapeutically relevant problems, where less is decided in advance. In these pilots, BigHat chose the targets and the types of molecule to optimize up front. Next, a scientist and Claude could be given a more open goal, such as finding a drug candidate for a particular disease, and work together to figure out what kind of molecule to make, which experiments to run, and what qualifies as success. A research team does its best work when it is aiming at the right goal, and in drug discovery, identifying the right metric early can save years of work and millions of dollars.

AI can make the search for better molecules faster. It can also help scientists understand how molecules work, and how to measure what they really want.

Enveda

Enveda: Streamlining document creation and review for drugs in clinical development

The Enveda team shares how they worked with Claude to create new tools for compiling and reviewing the complex regulatory documentation they prepare for active programs in clinical trials.

Read Enveda’s technical report and get in touch (info@enveda.com).

Enveda is a biotechnology company that seeks to discover new medicines from molecules living organisms have evolved to produce. The company hypothesizes that these natural molecules—the products of billions of years of evolution—form a highly effective chemical library for drug discovery. Natural molecules have already led to medicines such as aspirin and metformin, which are derived from plants, and GLP-1 drugs, which are derived from human chemistry.

Since 2019, Enveda has produced 17 drug candidates, three of which are now in clinical trials. Its goal is to develop new treatments for common conditions such as chronic inflammation, obesity, and cardiometabolic disease, as well as for reproductive and mental health conditions that have historically received less attention from the pharmaceutical industry.

Here, the Enveda team describes two projects: one in which they used Claude to help review manufacturing records, and one in which they used Claude to write first drafts of regulatory documents. In one evaluation, Enveda ran its Claude-powered review system multiple times on a set of records that human reviewers had also checked manually. Together, those runs raised all 11 of the issues the human reviewers had found. One of the runs, which Enveda timed, also raised seven issues that no human reviewer had flagged. Reviewing the records by hand took nearly 26 hours, whereas the timed run took less than three, including the time Enveda’s reviewers spent checking the system’s comments—an 89% reduction. These projects offer promising first signs that AI tools might speed up the process of document review, which is one of the bottlenecks to getting a new medicine approved.

  • Read Enveda’s technical report, “CDP: a language-model system for quality review of executed pharmaceutical batch records”

Addressing two bottlenecks in drug manufacturing: document quality and turnaround time

Drug manufacturing is regulated under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP), a set of standards drugmakers must meet to affirm their products are of consistently high quality. Meeting GMP standards requires organizations to maintain thousands of technical records, often spread across a dozen partners, describing every aspect of the work performed, from operating and analytical procedures to the equipment used and the data generated. Before a batch is released, a quality team reviews the manufacturing records and decides whether to release or reject the batch.

At Enveda, a single manufacturing chemist may spend roughly 75% of their time reviewing documents. In one case, a senior Enveda reviewer needed 10 hours to assess a single 500-page record from a contract manufacturer. Furthermore, as a drug advances through each development phase, the number of drug batches and the associated documentation continue to grow.

This led Enveda to ask whether a large language model, equipped with the right tools, could help its reviewers catch the kinds of issues an inspector would be looking for as well as a human reviewer, but faster.

Enveda’s answer to this question is the CMC Document Processor, or CDP. (“CMC” stands for Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls.) The CDP is an application built around Claude that raises quality issues for Enveda’s reviewers, who then decide whether to act on each finding. CDP is linked to all of the manufacturing documents in Enveda’s centralized database for regulatory compliance. As the documents are uploaded by the manufacturer, Claude automatically identifies the material and batch it relates to. The app then uses fixed rules to find the documents that govern that record, such as the approved manufacturing instructions and the material specification. Claude checks each page against any other relevant documents and flags possible errors, inconsistencies, and open questions. The model receives a read-only copy of each document and cannot write to the official record, which stays in Enveda’s validated document system.

Enveda used the CDP on four batch records totaling 1,061 pages from a Phase 2 manufacturing campaign that was later submitted to the FDA. To test the system’s performance, Enveda compared its results with two pairs of Enveda reviewers and two experienced external consultants who manually reviewed the records. Finally, a member of Enveda’s Quality department, who had not reviewed any of the records, scored every comment, human or AI, based on whether an auditor reading the same record would raise a particular issue. A second member of the department confirmed the scoring.

Enveda ran the CDP on these records more than once, but the findings they report here are from one timed run, which was also the first time Claude had seen any of the documentation from this program.

Across its runs on these records, Enveda found that the CDP raised each of the 11 issues that the human reviewers had found and that Enveda’s Quality team judged needed to be addressed. The timed run also raised seven further issues that needed to be addressed that no human reviewer had flagged, which Enveda’s Quality department resolved with the manufacturer before the batch was released. Enveda describes these as detail-level items, which call for a correction or a question to the manufacturer. Issues included a process step that ran over the time limit, and a test marked as failed where no result was recorded. As with every CDP comment, Enveda’s reviewers decided how to address each one, and the decision of whether to release a batch remained with Enveda’s Quality department.

Enveda’s review did not detect any hallucinated findings, as no CDP comment referred to content that was not in the records. In the timed run, the CDP did misread one entry, a struck-through handwritten correction. In this case, Claude noted that its comment warranted verification against the source data rather than presenting its assessment as fact. Most comments from both the CDP and the human reviewers needed no action: 33 of 42 (79%) for the CDP’s timed run, and 79 of 96 (82%) for the human reviewers. The timed run also raised fewer comments that needed no action: 33 compared with 79 from the human reviewers.

In this evaluation, review with the CDP also took less time. Enveda’s four reviewers spent 25.9 hours reviewing the four records by hand. Running the CDP and having the reviewers check its comments and citations took 2.8 hours, an 89% reduction for this use case. The CDP was additive to, rather than a replacement for, human review: Enveda’s Quality team still conducts its own final review of each record before deciding whether to release a batch.

Enveda has also used the CDP outside of this evaluation. In another drug program, the team ran it retrospectively on a draft one-month stability report, which tracks how a drug holds up in storage. The report covered more data than usual, including results from demonstration batches—trial production runs that aren’t made under GMP. In those batches, the level of one impurity had roughly tripled under “accelerated” conditions, where a drug is deliberately stored in harsher conditions to see how quickly it will degrade over time. Human reviewers hadn’t raised this as an issue and didn’t notice the rising impurity level trend because it was still within the approved limits. However, the CDP flagged the trend as a “Major” concern—and, indeed, in later testing, the impurity in the demonstration batch did rise above the approved limits. Spotting trends like this early can help prevent lost batches and costly clinical delays.

Read more about how Enveda built the CDP and their head-to-head test here.

Drafting part of an FDA application from a single source of truth

Before a drug candidate can be tested in humans, its sponsor must file an Investigational New Drug (IND) application with the FDA. The application follows a prescribed format and includes administrative information, summaries, quality modules, nonclinical and clinical data, the Investigator’s Brochure (which provides trial physicians with information about the drug), and the trial protocol. Like drug batch records, an IND application can span thousands of pages drawn from dozens of study reports, and every claim must be traceable to its source data. Because the same facts appear in several documents, keeping them consistent takes care. Errors in the paperwork can be costly: in 2019, for example, the FDA refused to even begin reviewing an application for an epilepsy drug, in part because it contained the wrong version of a clinical dataset.

Enveda worked with an Anthropic engineer to set up a workflow in which Claude drafted significant portions of an IND application—specifically Module 2, which summarizes the application’s quality, nonclinical, and clinical data. Claude worked from source documents containing toxicology, bioanalytical, and pharmacokinetic studies.

The project’s most important design choice was to add an intermediate step to the data-gathering process. Rather than asking Claude to write each document straight from the study reports, Enveda first had Claude extract the key facts from those reports into a shared, structured dataset. The team could then build every document from this single source of truth. When an analysis or fact required refinement, the team could update it consistently across every relevant instance in the draft.

To check the work, Enveda compared Claude’s draft with an IND Module 2 that its team had drafted manually before the Anthropic collaboration began. Enveda’s subject matter and quality assurance experts reviewed the drafts for accuracy, adherence to Enveda’s style guide and templates, and suitability for submission. Enveda’s Drug Development and Quality teams deemed Claude’s draft comparable to work produced by professional medical writers, and would have felt comfortable submitting it if the human-written version hadn’t already been sent to the publishers.

Claude generated the first draft in four hours. Enveda says that writing a first draft of the same content manually took approximately three months. Neither figure includes the three-week manual review process that every draft goes through.

In parallel, Enveda worked with Claude to draft an Investigator’s Brochure from the same source materials. Enveda’s reviewers found one minor error, involving a unit conversion in a toxicology study. Once that was fixed, Enveda’s Drug Development and Quality teams deemed the brochure acceptable for submission, and the document was submitted to the FDA.

In both cases, Claude supported Enveda’s experts rather than replacing established quality processes. Enveda reports that after three internal hackathons, most of its technical team can now generate first drafts of IND content themselves, which the company says gives them more time to prepare other regulatory submissions and develop protocols for additional programs.

What Enveda learned

Drug regulation holds the development of new medicines to exceptionally high standards of quality and safety in order to protect patients. Meeting those standards requires producing and reviewing a large volume of regulated documents by hand. But even the most careful reviewers can miss details.

In Enveda’s experience, off-the-shelf AI models struggled to review and generate regulated documents, which are highly technical and nuanced. The team found that existing Claude models could support this work once they were given the right workflow, context, and instructions, with Enveda’s experts reviewing the outputs and making every final decision. Combining their own pharmaceutical domain expertise with support from an Anthropic engineer, Enveda built the CDP and IND drafting tools in a few weeks. Their main takeaway from these projects is that when a team configures the tools correctly and incorporates deep domain expertise, models like Claude can dramatically improve the speed, cost, and quality of certain parts of drug development.

The Column Group (TCG)

How Claude is helping The Column Group reimagine the biotech company

The Column Group (TCG) is a venture capital firm that has invested in biotechnology companies since 2007. The group explores opportunities in drug discovery that have the potential to bring novel medicines to patients with serious unmet medical needs. The firm believes AI will be transformative for the biotech industry, and that AI-enabled biotech companies are the future to unlocking novel drug discovery.

Get in touch (info@thecolumngroup.com).

Drug discovery and development are slow and expensive, with high failure rates that only get more expensive at each stage of the process. Much of the most innovative drug discovery work falls to small biotech companies, which depend heavily on private investors for funding.

Because the discovery process is so expensive and time-consuming, investors tend to be cautious about which initiatives they fund: they typically want to see quick returns, so they often favor research that has been validated by others, or programs that can quickly generate positive clinical data. Unfortunately, this steers the ecosystem away from investing in designing novel drugs against new targets in diseases where there is significant unmet need.

Recent advancements in AI could fundamentally change the incentive structures driving the industry and potentially rebalance the investor appetite for novel biology. For instance, efficiency gains in time and money from using frontier tools and agents could reduce the time and financial risks involved in investing in cutting-edge research.

Biotech executives want to spend more time on challenging disease biology questions and less time weighing how to finance high-risk, high-reward endeavors. But making that happen requires a concerted effort to integrate frontier models and workflows across a diverse ecosystem of biotech companies that each focus on a variety of research methods, biologies, and therapeutic areas.

This is where TCG and Anthropic see an opportunity to reimagine what a biotech company can look like.

The challenges limiting innovation in biotech

Unlike other industries—such as software development or medical tech—drug development in biotech lacks methods for efficient prototyping. It’s not possible to generate a minimally viable product, gather feedback from users, and iterate to find product-market fit. In biotech, researchers start with the outline of an ideally designed product from the beginning. And because it’s so expensive and time-consuming to both design drugs and test them in clinical trials, most companies have only one shot to get things right—maybe two, if they’re lucky.

The challenge is even greater when researching novel targets—sets of proteins or genes that nobody has previously designed a drug against. This work has enormous potential, but introduces significant risks and unknowns. In order to successfully develop a drug against a novel target, a company must:

  • Identify an unmet medical need that will still be unmet five to 10 years from now;
  • Evaluate what earlier researchers have discovered about the biological processes driving the condition in question;
  • Determine whether there’s a real opportunity to alter those processes in a way that can improve patient outcomes;
  • Invent a product that can operate on the underlying biology in a way that’s safe, effective, and manufacturable at a reasonable cost to patients; and
  • Design and execute a clinical study to robustly and affordably test the therapeutic hypothesis.

Building the biotech model for the future

TCG scientists are working with Anthropic engineers and Claude to streamline some of those industry bottlenecks, with the ultimate goal of building an AI-forward business model that any biotech company could replicate.

Bringing AI into biotech won’t be easy. Historically, biotechs have been built like small villages, each focusing on its own particular set of specialities. This makes sense—the types of expertise required to develop a drug for asthma are very different from the expertise required for tackling migraines or cancer, and companies that focus on any one of these diseases need different kinds of resources, workflows, and scientists.

But without a standard model for AI in biotech, the challenge is to build something that works for the whole industry while satisfying the particular needs of each company within it. Application across this landscape will require AI agents for specialized tasks as well as scientists and operators from diverse backgrounds who know how to use them well. This is understandably a sprawling process.

To tackle the sprawl, TCG is building Pleiades, a new kind of biotech company.

Pleiades is being made in collaboration with Anthropic engineers to be fully AI-enabled from Day One. The mission is to capture the full power and efficiency of AI for Pleiades’s programs while serving as a laboratory for a biotech AI toolkit—it builds the agents, skills, and workflows that any biotech company would need, deployed across every company TCG creates from here forward.

The core scientific and operational tasks needed to maximize the efficiency of drug discovery in Pleiades are perfectly suited for AI systems like Claude. Pleiades and TCG scientists will work with Claude to generate, evaluate, and derisk new research ideas, to speed up the initiation of novel drug discovery programs. Our scientists will then work with Claude on molecular design, medicinal chemistry, and planning and executing experiments from the start of the program, eventually helping test a drug’s efficacy in vivo and later in clinical trials. Collectively, this could enable a small drug discovery team to run more programs than they could before. It could also help with time-consuming administrative tasks, such as coordinating with vendors, accounting, forecasting, project planning, and recruiting.

The Claude-enabled scientific capabilities will help Pleiades evaluate potential risks associated with pursuing any hypothesis much earlier, streamlining and improving the quality of early decision-making, before too much time and money have been spent going after a flawed idea. The savings will enable previously untenable risks on a larger array of challenging and innovative projects.

Leveraging Claude to increase the speed and quality of early decision-making

TCG scientists have been working with Anthropic engineers to build tools for scientific and operational work at every stage of the development pipeline.

The first collection of Claude tools generate and evaluate novel biological hypotheses and targets for patients with life-changing diseases and few therapeutic options. This process begins with TCG’s scientists, who test every initial hypothesis for a new drug by reading papers, patents, and competitor disclosures, and by talking to key scientists and clinicians in the field. In TCG’s experience, each project has required a champion to assemble the information required to evaluate the therapeutic potential and competitive landscape. This initial target assessment can take anywhere from hours to days, and completing a full investigation before committing to an investment often takes months. As a result, the number of new projects TCG can explore is limited by each scientist’s expertise and time.

TCG’s scientists and Anthropic’s engineers are developing a Claude Skill (a package of instructions and resources that Claude can load for a specific task) to speed up this initial target assessment. The teams direct Claude to investigate a paired molecular target and disease; Claude then assigns dozens of subagents to find and synthesize published research on biology, human genetics, past trials, chemistry, and competitive projects, and generates a report that analyzes the pros and cons of pursuing the respective drug discovery program. A TCG scientist then reads the report, investigates key pieces of evidence that support Claude’s conclusions, and decides whether the idea is sufficiently compelling to merit further investigation.

TCG has evaluated the Skill on 45 disease-target pairs spanning known and novel targets, and found that combining the Skill’s output with internal expert judgment, as opposed to relying on expert review alone, has cut the process of finding and interpreting the relevant scientific literature from weeks to days. Importantly, this has improved the quality and focus of each individual’s time between initial target consideration and deciding whether to pursue deeper work on the target.

The Skill has already been deployed across TCG and is currently being used to evaluate new target ideas. While our work is still early, these results are an important step toward demonstrating Claude’s ability to speed up this initial step of the drug discovery process, so scientists and investors can focus on the most important questions to answer before launching a drug discovery project.

TCG is also working with Anthropic to develop a Claude Skill for clinical strategy. Among the many reasons drug discovery can fail is that it didn’t identify an effective strategy for a clinical trial early on. Most drugs that enter clinical trials never reach approval, often because of weaknesses in trial strategies that only become apparent after years of work and many millions of dollars. Even though trials typically take place years after a drug discovery program begins, deciding on a strong strategy at the beginning is essential. If one can’t test a drug in clinical trials, there’s no point developing it at all. Diligence in this area involves exploring a huge number of variables, including which clinical outcomes should be measured in patients, regulatory pathways, patient population, site selection, and safety monitoring. Experts typically don’t have time to investigate all of these options fully at the program initiation stage.

For the clinical strategy Skill, TCG and Anthropic collaborated to identify the key clinical trial variables that Claude needed to consider, and selected test scenarios where they had the clinical expertise to evaluate Claude’s outputs. TCG then worked with Claude to test assumptions around a target-indication match, refine disease and patient-selection strategy, and lay out the key unknowns that underlie the clinical development risk for each program.

Since TCG started building this skill in August, the firm has evaluated clinical development strategy decisions across targets in cancer, cardiometabolic diseases, and inflammation and immunology (I&I). In oncology, the Skill has outlined the evidence to support what the most compelling combination studies are for an internal portfolio asset, accounting for both the current standard of care and the other drugs in development approaching approval. In I&I, the Skill has been used to choose between different diseases that the drug might be tested in, accounting for the need to balance time, cost and demonstration of clinical efficacy to drive value creation. One of the programs evaluated with this Skill is the first program nominated in Pleiades. In the future, TCG hopes to use this Skill to select new drug hypotheses primarily on the basis of a creative clinical strategy.

Better tools are only a first step toward better medicines

TCG and Anthropic are building these tools to help lower costs in drug discovery so that patients can get the treatments they need sooner. This requires pairing biotech innovation and new entrepreneurship strategies with frontier AI expertise—that’s what Pleiades, built with Anthropic, is designed to do. Together, TCG and Anthropic will bring AI into the ecosystem faster than any company could alone.

Mithril Medicine

Mithril Medicine: Pipeline-in-a-Person

Cody Tranbarger at Mithril Medicine shares how he used Claude to streamline early-stage drug research, working with the model across experiment design, planning, and analysis.

Read our technical report.

Mithril Medicine is developing treatments for two rare and particularly aggressive forms of muscular dystrophy: LAMA2-related congenital muscular dystrophy and α-dystroglycanopathy.

Before it was acquired by Anthropic in July, Mithril had only one full-time employee: its founder, Cody Tranbarger. The rest of the work was done by part-time consultants and third-party labs. But Cody found that the team’s progress was often held back by the logistical burdens of scientific research, including coordinating vendors, analyzing data, and planning next steps. He began using Claude extensively to ease those bottlenecks.

By offloading much of the company’s operational tasks to Claude, Mithril was able to develop a proof-of-principle for an antibody treatment targeting LAMA2-CMD in less than 18 months. In their short-term study, mice with a severe form of the disease that received the antibody therapy had roughly twice the grip strength and tricep force of untreated mice, and more of them survived to the end of the study.

Mithril’s work on these two diseases became Anthropic’s first in-house drug development programs. It’s also been Anthropic’s first experiment with this “Pipeline-in-a-Person” research model, in which one scientist works with Claude to pursue several biological hypotheses at the same time. Cody still runs both of Mithril’s original research programs, and is preparing to start several more. Beyond Mithril, turning the first idea for a new drug program into a detailed thesis, design plan and experimental strategy took two to three days. Cody estimates that, without Claude, it would have taken him one to two months.

These are early results in mice, not human trials, so these results don’t show that the treatment is safe or effective in patients.

Protecting muscle fibers with bispecific antibodies

LAMA2-related congenital muscular dystrophy (LAMA2-CMD) affects an estimated 4,000–5,000 people in the US and Europe. Most patients are severely weak from birth and never walk on their own. Many need ventilators and feeding tubes, and few live to the age of 30. There are no approved treatments; only one drug has ever been tested, but development was halted in 2021 after showing no sign of working. There are a handful of others in development, but none have reached clinical trials.

In our muscles, each fiber is held in place by a protein sheet, which protects it from tearing as the muscle tightens and relaxes. The fiber is anchored to the sheet by a bond between two proteins: laminin, in the sheet, and dystroglycan, in the muscle cell’s outer membrane.

Children with LAMA2-CMD inherit a faulty gene from both parents that prevents their bodies from making the usual form of laminin. Their bodies make a related form in its place, but this stand-in grips dystroglycan only weakly. In α-dystroglycanopathy (αDG), the dystroglycan protein isn’t able to hold on to laminin. In both diseases, the weak anchor leaves muscle fibers fragile; they are easily damaged by movement and, over time, the bonds between the sheet and the muscle fibers are so weak that the muscle eventually dies and is replaced by fat and scar tissue.

Mithril is attempting to rebuild broken anchors with antibodies, Y-shaped proteins naturally produced by the immune system. Most antibody drugs work chemically: they block a signal, or switch one on, or mark a cell for the immune system to destroy. Mithril’s antibodies are bispecific, meaning each binds to two different targets. One arm grabs dystroglycan on the muscle fiber, while the other grabs laminin on the surrounding sheet. The resulting bridge is designed to work like a clamp, locking the fiber onto its sheet. In LAMA2-CMD, the second arm grips the stand-in laminin that can only bind weakly by itself. In αDG, it grips the normal laminin that the faulty dystroglycan can’t hold.

There are good reasons to expect this strategy to work. Mithril isn’t the first lab to test something like this: previous research has shown that LAMA2-CMD patients who still make small amounts of normal laminin have much later onset and much milder symptoms, which suggests that even a partial repair could help patients. In mice with LAMA2-CMD, synthetic molecules that restored the link between dystroglycan and laminin improved the animals’ muscles and quadrupled their median lifespan; and another team has shown that an antibody of this kind improved muscle function in mice with αDG.

What they found

Before joining Anthropic, Mithril tested the idea in mice. It used “tool molecules”—early, unrefined versions of its antibodies made to test whether the approach works at all. The mice were engineered to have a severe form of LAMA2-CMD resembling human disease. Without treatment, the mice die within weeks of birth.

The antibodies went where they were needed: stained muscles from treated mice showed the molecules spread throughout the tissue and sitting at the surface of the muscle fibers, where the broken anchor must be repaired. Untreated muscle showed no sign of the drug.

After six weeks, mice on the highest dose had roughly twice the grip strength and muscle force of untreated ones. Survival improved as well: between two and six weeks of age, one of eight treated mice died, against four of eight untreated mice, while all 12 healthy mice lived. The treated mice remained weaker than healthy mice, so the treatment was not a cure, but it was a large improvement, achieved with a minimum viable product version of the drug in one of the hardest mouse models of the disease—solid evidence that the core idea is sound.

A new way of working

Analysis of drug companies suggests the most productive are distinguished by their taste and judgement of what research topics to pursue. But sound judgement takes time and attention, and much of drug hunters’ time is spent on admin and logistics. At Mithril, reagents, documents, data, and decisions moved between 11 outside labs, and Cody had to keep track of it all. The load grows with every new program, which limits how many programs one person can run.

All of this makes drug development slower and more expensive, and it is a particular problem for rare diseases. If a condition affects only a few thousand people, a conventional research team can be too costly to justify, and so many such diseases go neglected. Freeing up drug hunters’ time will both improve overall research productivity and make it economically viable to target a wider range of diseases.

Mithril’s work reveals one way in which AI can help, by changing how drug companies are organized.

Claude handled much of the logistical work of running the program, requiring only high-level human guidance. When responsibility for a key set of experiments changed hands, Claude pieced together where things stood from email threads and 14 months of slide decks. It worked out what was holding things up and drafted five handover emails, which Cody reviewed and sent to two outside labs. When the labs’ antibody screening came back as hundreds of separate files, Claude combined them into a single list of promising candidates. Within a few hours, after a single prompt from Cody, it had also assessed how easily each one could be developed into a drug.

Keeping track of the outside labs, and making sense of what they send back, is where Claude saved Cody the most time. At Anthropic, Mithril is testing how far that can go: whether a single drug hunter can direct several programs at once. In this Pipeline-in-a-Person model, Claude keeps the record of each program, coordinates the outside labs, and keeps work moving. The human sets the scientific direction and makes the decisions: which experiments to run, whether a program continues or stops, and what gets signed off.

There are still hard limits on how quickly biology can be done; Claude can’t make cells grow or mice age any faster. But with the logistics handled, one person can test more ideas in parallel, and make better decisions along the way. More ideas, better drugs, sooner and cheaper—that is what Claude can enable today. For ultra-rare diseases, where it is rarely feasible to pay a conventional, full-sized team, that could decide which medicines get made at all. Mithril is the first test of whether the model works on real programs.

What’s next

While the mouse study was running, Mithril also found novel antibody arms that bind to each of its targets. It has now built more than 100 new bispecific antibodies, pairing each arm that grabs laminin with each arm that grabs dystroglycan. These are currently being tested in cell-based experiments. The aim is to prioritize a few of the strongest by the end of 2026, and to name formal drug candidates for both diseases in 2027.

If the drug candidates chosen in 2027 hold up, Mithril aims to start clinical trials in LAMA2-CMD as soon as late 2028, and in αDG as soon as 2029. Its stated goal is for the children born with LAMA2-CMD today to be the last who grow up without an approved treatment.

Cody is also getting ready to start several more programs, all directed toward rare pediatric diseases with no approved therapy. Each new program will test further whether a single scientist, working with Claude, can run what amounts to a pipeline of drugs. Anthropic plans to say more in the coming months about how it hopes to bring this way of working to other drug hunters.
